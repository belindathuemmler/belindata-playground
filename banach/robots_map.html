The Robot's Map

A robot 🤖 in a small room wants to reach its charging station 🔋. It can move up, down, left or right. Every move costs 1 battery point, bumping into a wall too; at the station it gains 2 points per tick. Before it walks, it thinks. In its head it keeps a map with one number per cell: how good is it to stand here? At first the map is all zeros. Each round of thinking applies the Bellman operator once: every cell looks at its best neighbour and computes \(-1 + 0.8 \cdot (\text{value of the best neighbour})\).

Big number: the robot's map Arrows: where it would go (? = no idea yet) Grey: the true value
How wrong is the map?
Why the error shrinks

Compare the robot's map with the truth in any cell:

map  : −1 + 0.8 · V(best neighbour)
truth: −1 + 0.8 · V*(best neighbour)

The −1 is the same on both sides and cancels. What remains: the new error is at most 0.8 times the largest old error. That is the contraction, and Banach turns it into a guarantee: the map converges to the truth, whatever it started with.

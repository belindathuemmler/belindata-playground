Hausdorff’s Recipe, Step by Step

A cover is a handful of balls that together hide the object. The objects sit in space, like the pencil line on the kitchen scale: a thread (one-dimensional) and a twisted band (two-dimensional). Every ball has diameter δ and counts the way an \(s\)-dimensional ball of that size would: its length, its area, its volume, which is \((\text{diameter})^s\) up to a constant. Shrink the balls and watch the three sums. This is the table from above, computed.

Drag to turn the room.

The 3D view needs WebGL, which this browser has switched off. The numbers below still work.

Watch the count: each time \(\delta\) halves, the thread needs twice as many balls, the band four times as many. That growth rate is the dimension. The three prices below only test which \(s\) balances it: the sum is \(N \cdot (\text{price})\), roughly \(\delta^{-\dim} \cdot \delta^{s}\), and it stays put exactly when \(s\) matches.

s = 1
each ball counts δ, like a length
s = 2
each ball counts πδ²⁄4, like an area
s = 3
each ball counts πδ³⁄6, like a volume

This is one sensible cover; Hausdorff takes the cheapest of all. That is why the band settles near \(\pi/2\) times its area here, and at its area in his measure. The cheapest cover changes the number, never whether it explodes, vanishes or stays put.
